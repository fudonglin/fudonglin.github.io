#  Simplify Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) via Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

Post-training is a critical step for aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human preferences—making them more helpful, honest, safe, and instruction-following. The dominant approach has been Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), most notably with [Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347), which played an important role in aligning models such as InstructGPT and GPT-4. However, PPO-based RLHF is notoriously complex and computationally expensive. It typically requires multiple components, including a policy model, value model, reward model, and reference model, along with careful stabilization techniques during training.

[Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.18290) offers a compelling alternative. Instead of explicitly training a reward model and running reinforcement learning, DPO directly optimizes the language model using preference pairs through a simple classification loss. This makes DPO much simpler and more efficient than PPO-based RLHF, especially for LLM alignment under limited hardware and computational resources.

> Before diving in DPO, I recommend reading my previous [PPO blog](https://fudonglin.github.io/2026/04/29/ppo.html), which introduces the basics of reinforcement learning, RLHF, and the core mechanics of PPO.



## Preliminaries

### KL Divergence

KL divergence is a concept from information theory that measures how different one probability distribution is from a reference distribution. In other words, it tells us how much information is lost when we approximate a true distribution $P$ with another distribution $Q$.

For a discrete probability distribution, KL divergence is defined as:

$$
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q)
=
\sum_x P(x)\log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}.
$$

For a continuous probability distribution, we can write KL divergence as an expectation:

$$
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}
\left[
\log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}
\right].
$$

A key property of KL divergence is that it is not symmetric:

$$
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q)
\neq
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \parallel P).
$$

This means the order of the two distributions matters. $\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q)$ measures how much $Q$ differs from $P$, assuming samples are drawn from $P$.



### Bradley-Terry Model

Given a single prompt and two LLM responses (completions), a pairwise preference is a binary signal—assigned by a human annotator or an LLM judge—indicating which response is preferred. Let $\mathcal{D} = \left\{x^{(i)},\; y_c^{(i)},\; y_r^{(i)}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}$ denote a human preference dataset, where $x^{(i)}$ is the prompt, $y_c^{(i)}$ is the chosen (preferred) response, $y_r^{(i)}$ is the rejected response, and $N$ is the total number of preference pairs. According to the [Bradley-Terry model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradley–Terry_model), the probability that $y_c$ is preferred over $y_r$ given prompt $x$ is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(y_c \succ y_r \mid x) =
\frac{
\exp\left(r(x, y_c)\right)
}{
\exp\left(r(x, y_c)\right)
+
\exp\left(r(x, y_r)\right)
}
\end{aligned},
$$

where $r(x,y)$ is a scalar preference score over the prompt-completion pair. In practice, we train a Reward Model (RM) to assign the preference score.



### Reward Model

A reward model (RM) $r_\theta(x, y)$ is a decoder-only transformer augmented with an appended linear classification head, which takes a prompt–completion pair $(x, y)$ as input and outputs a scalar score reflecting how preferred the completion is. The architecture is illustrated below.

![reward_model](https://github.com/fudonglin/fudonglin.github.io/blob/main/_posts/2026-05-10-dpo/reward_model.png?raw=true)

Image credit: [Deep (Learning) Focus](https://cameronrwolfe.substack.com/p/direct-preference-optimization).

Given a preference dataset, we train the RM to produce scores consistent with the observed human preferences under the Bradley-Terry model— that is, we want $r_\theta$ to assign higher scores to chosen responses than to rejected ones, across all preference pairs. We do this by minimizing the negative log-likelihood via maximum likelihood estimation (MLE):

$$
\mathcal{L}(\phi) = -\log \left( \sigma \left( r_{\phi}(x, y_c) - r_{\phi}(x, y_r) \right) \right),
$$

where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid function.



## Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) 

![PPO_arch](https://github.com/fudonglin/fudonglin.github.io/blob/main/_posts/2026-05-10-dpo/PPO_arch.png?raw=true)

Figure : The PPO training pipeline for LLM alignment, consisting of four models: a trained policy model, a frozen reference model, a frozen reward model, and a trained value model. Image credit: [DeepSeekMath](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300).



Once a reward model is trained, PPO-based RLHF fine-tunes the language model (the *policy* $\pi_\theta$) to maximize the expected reward while staying close to a reference model $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Formally, the training objective is:

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_\theta(y|x)} \left[ r_\phi(x, y) - \beta \,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[\pi_\theta(y \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\right] \right].
$$

The KL penalty prevents the policy from drifting too far from the reference model and avoids degenerate solutions that exploit the reward model. As illustrated in the above figure, the PPO training pipeline involves four models running simultaneously:

- **Policy Model** $\pi_\theta$: the LLM being trained
- **Reference Model** $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$: a frozen copy of the initial policy, used to compute the KL penalty
- **Reward Model** $r_\phi$: frozen after pre-training, provides the scalar reward signal
- **Value Model** $V_\phi$: trained alongside the policy to estimate expected returns for the PPO update



### Limitations of PPO-Based RLHF

While PPO-based RLHF has proven effective for LLM alignment, it comes with two significant limitations:

**Computationally Demanding.** PPO requires maintaining and running inference on all four models simultaneously. This multi-model architecture leads to substantial GPU memory consumption and long training times, making it prohibitively expensive for most practitioners without access to large-scale compute infrastructure.

**Training Instability.** As a reinforcement learning algorithm, PPO is notoriously difficult to stabilize. The training process is sensitive to hyperparameter choices, reward scaling, and the quality of the reward model itself. A poorly calibrated reward model can lead to reward hacking, where the policy exploits unintended shortcuts to maximize reward rather than genuinely improving alignment. These instabilities often require careful tuning and multiple trial runs, further adding to cost and complexity.

These limitations motivate the need for a simpler, more stable alternative—which is precisely what DPO aims to address.





## Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

### DPO Objective Function

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) was proposed as a simpler and more efficient alternative to PPO-based RLHF. Its key insight is elegant: under the KL-constrained RLHF objective, the optimal policy has a closed-form analytical expression. This allows us to reparameterize the reward function directly in terms of the policy itself—eliminating the need for an explicit reward model entirely.

Specifically, the optimal policy takes the form (we will discuss this later):

$$
\pi^*(y \mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{r_\phi(x,y)}{\beta}\right),
$$

where $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r_\phi(x,y)}{\beta}\right)$ is the **partition function**—a normalizing constant that depends only on $x$, not on the policy $\pi_{\theta} (y \mid x)$.

Rearranging, the implicit reward can be written as:

$$
r_{\phi}(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
$$

Crucially, when we substitute this reparameterized reward into the Bradley-Terry preference model (Eq. 5), the intractable partition function $Z(x)$ **cancels out** in the difference between chosen and rejected rewards. Taking the negative log-likelihood then yields the DPO loss:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,\, y_c,\, y_r) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_c \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_c \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_r \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_r \mid x)}\right)\right].
$$

With this reparameterization, DPO reduces preference alignment to a supervised binary classification problem over chosen–rejected response pairs. A single loss simultaneously increases the likelihood of preferred responses and suppresses dispreferred ones—with no separate reward model training and no RL sampling loop required.

#### How Does DPO Work?

To understand what DPO is actually doing during training, it helps to examine its gradient. Taking the derivative of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}$ with respect to $\theta$ yields: 

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta) = -\beta\, \mathbb{E}_{(x,\, y_c,\, y_r) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \underbrace{  \sigma\!\left(    \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_r \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_r \mid x)}    - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_c \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_c \mid x)}  \right) }_{\text{adaptive weight} \in (0,1)} \cdot \left(  \underbrace{\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_c \mid x)}_{\text{increase chosen likelihood}}  - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_r \mid x)}_{\text{decrease rejected likelihood}} \right) \right].
$$

The table below lists the role of each component.

| Component                                                    | Role                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| $\sigma\!\left(\beta \log \dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_r \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}(y_r \mid x)} - \beta \log \dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_c \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}(y_c \mid x)}\right)$ | **Adaptive weight**: near 1 when the model is "wrong" (rewards rejected > chosen), near 0 when already correct |
| $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_c \mid x)$                  | Push up the probability of the **chosen** response           |
| $-\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_r \mid x)$                 | Push down the probability of the **rejected** response       |

Intuitively, the gradient decomposes into two interpretable components:

- **Direction:** The direction of the update always pushes the policy to assign higher likelihood to $y_c$ and lower likelihood to $y_r$—the correct direction for preference learning. 
- **Magnitude:** The magnitude of each update is modulated by an adaptive weight: the sigmoid of the implicit reward gap between the rejected and chosen responses. This weight is close to 1 when the model is miscalibrated (assigning higher implicit reward to $y_r$ than to $y_c$), and shrinks toward 0 when the model already strongly prefers $y_c$. DPO therefore concentrates learning on the preference pairs where the model is most wrong—analogous to how a classifier naturally down-weights easy examples. 



### Deriving the Optimal Policy for RLHF

Before deriving the DPO loss, we first establish the closed-form optimal policy for the KL-constrained RLHF objective:

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_\theta(y|x)} \left[ r_\phi(x, y) - \beta \,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[\pi_\theta(y \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\right] \right].
$$

The goal is to find the policy $\pi^*$ that maximizes expected reward while remaining close to the reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. We derive the solution by algebraically transforming this objective into a KL divergence minimization:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\max_{\pi_\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x,\, y \sim \pi_\theta}
\left[
r_{\phi}(x,y)
- \beta \,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) \parallel \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\right)
\right]
&& \text{Original KL-constrained objective}
\\[6pt]
&= \max_{\pi_\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x,\, y \sim \pi_\theta}
\left[
r_{\phi}(x,y) - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)}
\right]
&& \text{Expand the KL divergence}
\\[6pt]
&= \min_{\pi_\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x,\, y \sim \pi_\theta}
\left[
\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)} - r_{\phi}(x,y)
\right]
&& \text{Negate and flip max to min}
\\[6pt]
&\equiv \min_{\pi_\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x,\, y \sim \pi_\theta}
\left[
\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)} - \frac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x,y)
\right]
&& \text{Divide through by } \beta
\\[6pt]
&= \min_{\pi_\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x,\, y \sim \pi_\theta}
\left[
\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x) \cdot \exp\!\left(\frac{r_{\phi}(x,y)}{\beta}\right)}
\right]
&& \text{Use } a = \log\exp(a) \text{ and combine logs}
\\[6pt]
&= \min_{\pi_\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x,\, y \sim \pi_\theta}
\left[
\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x) \cdot \exp\!\left(\frac{r_{\phi}(x,y)}{\beta}\right)}
+ \log Z(x) - \log Z(x)
\right]
&& \text{Add and subtract } \log Z(x)
\\[6pt]
&= \min_{\pi_\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x,\, y \sim \pi_\theta}
\left[
\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x) \cdot \exp\!\left(\frac{r_{\phi}(x,y)}{\beta}\right)}
\right]
- \mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\log Z(x)\right]
&& \text{Absorb } Z(x) \text{ into the denominator}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r_\phi(x,y)}{\beta}\right)$ is the partition function—a normalizing constant that depends only on $x$, not on $\pi$.

This final form is the key insight. Define:

$$
\pi^*(y \mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{r_\phi(x,y)}{\beta}\right).
$$

The objective then becomes:

$$
\min_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\!\left[\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta} (y \mid x) \,\|\, \pi^*(y \mid x)\right)\right] - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\!\left[\log Z(x)\right].
$$

Since $\log Z(x)$ does not depend on $\pi$, minimizing over $\pi_{\theta}$ reduces to minimizing the KL divergence between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi^*$. KL divergence is non-negative and equals zero if and only if $\pi_{\theta} = \pi^*$ almost everywhere—so **the unique optimal policy is $\pi^*$ itself**.



### Deriving the Implicit Reward

Given the optimal policy $\pi^*$, we can rearrange to express the reward $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ purely in terms of policy log-ratios:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi^{*}(y \mid x)
&= \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r_{\phi}(x,y)}{\beta}\right)
&& \text{Optimal policy expression}
\\[6pt]
\log \pi^{*}(y \mid x)
&= \log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x) + \frac{1}{\beta}\,r_{\phi}(x,y) - \log Z(x)
&& \text{Take log of both sides, expand}
\\[6pt]
\frac{1}{\beta}\,r_{\phi}(x,y)
&= \log \pi^{*}(y \mid x) - \log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x) + \log Z(x)
&& \text{Isolate the reward term}
\\[6pt]
r_{\phi}(x,y)
&= \beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)
&& \text{Implicit reward function}
\end{aligned}
$$

This expression shows that the reward is fully determined by the log-ratio of the optimal policy to the reference policy, up to the additive constant $\beta \log Z(x)$. The partition function $Z(x)$ depends only on $x$ — and as we will see next, it cancels out entirely when computing the difference in rewards between a chosen and a rejected response, which is precisely what the Bradley-Terry model requires.



### Deriving the DPO Loss via the Bradley-Terry Model

With the implicit reward in hand, we now substitute it into the Bradley-Terry preference model to derive the DPO loss. Recall from Eq. (5) that the Bradley-Terry model defines the probability that response $y_c$ is preferred over $y_r$ given prompt $x$ as:

$$
P(y_c \succ y_r \mid x) = \sigma\!\left(r_{\phi}(x, y_c) - r_{\phi}(x, y_r)\right).
$$

Substituting the implicit reward $r_{\phi}(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$ into the reward difference:

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_{\phi}(x, y_c) - r_{\phi}(x, y_r)
&= \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_c \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_c \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right)
- \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_r \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_r \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right) \\[6pt]
&= \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_c \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_c \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_r \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_r \mid x)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The $\beta \log Z(x)$ terms cancel exactly, eliminating the intractable partition function. Replacing $\pi^*$ with the trainable policy $\pi_\theta$, the preference probability becomes:

$$
P(y_c \succ y_r \mid x) = \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_c \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_c \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_r \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_r \mid x)}\right).
$$

**DPO Loss.** Given a preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_c^{(i)}, y_r^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}$, we train $\pi_\theta$ by maximizing the likelihood of the observed preferences—equivalently, minimizing the negative log-likelihood:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,\, y_c,\, y_r) \sim \mathcal{D}}\!\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_c \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_c \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_r \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_r \mid x)}\right)\right].
$$

It has a clean intuitive interpretation: the loss is low when the trained policy $\pi_\theta$ assigns relatively higher likelihood to the chosen response $y_c$ over the rejected response $y_r$, compared to the reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. The hyperparameter $\beta > 0$ controls how aggressively the policy is pushed away from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ — a smaller $\beta$ allows larger updates, while a larger $\beta$ keeps the policy more conservative.

Crucially, no reward model needs to be trained, no RL sampling loop is required, and the intractable $Z(x)$ never appears in the final objective. DPO has effectively reduced the full RLHF pipeline to a single supervised loss over preference pairs.



### How Does DPO Yield an Optimal Policy?

A natural question arises: the implicit reward derived in the previous section $r_{\phi}(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$ contains the prompt-dependent term $\beta \log Z(x)$. Does this extra term cause any problems? The answer is no—and the reason lies in a fundamental property of reward equivalence.

**Definition (Reward Equivalence).** Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are equivalent if and only if their difference, for some function $f(\cdot)$, depends only on the prompt $x$, not on the response $y$:

$$
r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x).
$$

In other words, equivalent rewards differ only by a prompt-level shift that is constant across all completions.

This definition matters because both the Bradley-Terry preference model and the KL-constrained RLHF objective are insensitive to such prompt-level shifts—equivalent rewards produce identical preference distributions and identical optimal policies. We prove each claim in turn.

**Claim 1: Equivalent rewards yield the same preference distribution.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{r}(y_c \succ y_r \mid x)
&= \frac{
\exp\!\left(r(x,y_c)\right)
}{
\exp\!\left(r(x,y_c)\right) + \exp\!\left(r(x,y_r)\right)
}
&& \text{Bradley-Terry model under } r
\\[8pt]
&= \frac{
\exp\!\left(r'(x,y_c) + f(x)\right)
}{
\exp\!\left(r'(x,y_c) + f(x)\right) + \exp\!\left(r'(x,y_r) + f(x)\right)
}
&& \text{Apply reward equivalence}
\\[8pt]
&= \frac{
\exp\!\left(r'(x,y_c)\right)\exp\!\left(f(x)\right)
}{
\exp\!\left(r'(x,y_c)\right)\exp\!\left(f(x)\right) + \exp\!\left(r'(x,y_r)\right)\exp\!\left(f(x)\right)
}
&& \text{Use } \exp(a+b)=\exp(a)\exp(b)
\\[8pt]
&= \frac{
\exp\!\left(f(x)\right)\exp\!\left(r'(x,y_c)\right)
}{
\exp\!\left(f(x)\right)\!\left[\exp\!\left(r'(x,y_c)\right) + \exp\!\left(r'(x,y_r)\right)\right]
}
&& \text{Factor out } \exp\!\left(f(x)\right)
\\[8pt]
&= \frac{
\exp\!\left(r'(x,y_c)\right)
}{
\exp\!\left(r'(x,y_c)\right) + \exp\!\left(r'(x,y_r)\right)
}
&& \text{Cancel the shared factor}
\\[8pt]
&= P_{r'}(y_c \succ y_r \mid x)
&& \text{Bradley-Terry model under } r'.
\end{aligned}
$$

The prompt-level shift $f(x)$ cancels in the numerator and denominator, leaving the preference probability unchanged. 

**Claim 2: Equivalent rewards yield the same optimal policy.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi^{*}_{r}(y \mid x)
&= \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{r_\phi(x,y)}{\beta}\right)
&& \text{Optimal policy expression}
\\[8pt]
&= \frac{
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)
}{
\sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)
}
&& \text{Expand the partition function } Z(x)
\\[8pt]
&= \frac{
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r'(x,y) + f(x)}{\beta}\right)
}{
\sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r'(x,y) + f(x)}{\beta}\right)
}
&& \text{Apply reward equivalence}
\\[8pt]
&= \frac{
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r'(x,y)}{\beta}\right)\exp\!\left(\frac{f(x)}{\beta}\right)
}{
\sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r'(x,y)}{\beta}\right)\exp\!\left(\frac{f(x)}{\beta}\right)
}
&& \text{Use } \exp(a+b)=\exp(a)\exp(b)
\\[8pt]
&= \frac{
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r'(x,y)}{\beta}\right)
}{
\sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r'(x,y)}{\beta}\right)
}
&& \text{Cancel the shared factor } \exp\!\left(\frac{f(x)}{\beta}\right)
\\[8pt]
&= \pi^{*}_{r'}(y \mid x)
&& \text{Equivalent rewards induce the same optimal policy}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The prompt-level shift $\exp\!\left(\frac{f(x)}{\beta}\right)$ appears in both numerator and denominator and cancels exactly. 

**The implicit DPO reward is equivalent to the general reward.** The two claims above establish that reward equivalence is a harmless relationship—equivalent rewards are interchangeable in every sense that matters. It remains to show that the implicit DPO reward actually falls into the same equivalence class as the general reward $r(x,y)$.

To see this, define the modified reward:

$$
f(r)(x,y) = r(x,y) - \beta \log Z(x).
$$

Since the subtracted term $\beta \log Z(x)$ depends only on $x$ and not on $y$, the modified reward $f(r)(x,y)$ is equivalent to $r(x,y)$ by definition. Now substitute the implicit reward expression $r_{\phi}(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$ for $r(x,y)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(r)(x,y)
&= r(x,y) - \beta \log Z(x)
&& \text{Equivalent to the general reward } r(x,y)
\\[8pt]
&= \left[
\beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)
\right] - \beta \log Z(x)
&& \text{Substitute the implicit reward}
\\[8pt]
&= \beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)}
&& \text{Cancel } \beta \log Z(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the general reward $r(x,y)$ is equivalent to the implicit DPO reward:

$$
r_{\mathrm{DPO}}(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)}.
$$

Because $r(x,y)$ and $r_{\mathrm{DPO}}(x,y)$ are equivalent, Claims 1 and 2 guarantee that they produce the same preference distribution and the same optimal policy. DPO therefore optimizes the correct objective without ever needing to compute or estimate the intractable partition function $Z(x)$.



## Summary

### Difference between PPO and DPO

DPO offers a principled simplification of the RLHF pipeline that does not sacrifice alignment quality for efficiency—it simply recognizes that the complex RL objective has an elegant closed-form solution. By reparameterizing the implicit reward in terms of policy log-ratios, DPO eliminates the need for a separate reward model entirely and replaces the brittle RL training loop with a straightforward binary classification objective. The result is a method that is dramatically cheaper to run, easier to stabilize, and far simpler to implement—without requiring any fundamental compromise on the alignment goal.

| **Reward model**       | Explicitly trained in a separate step             | Implicitly defined via policy log-ratio |
| **Training paradigm**  | Reinforcement learning with on-policy sampling    | Supervised binary classification        |
| **Gradient update**    | PPO clip ratio, GAE, value loss                   | Standard gradient descent               |
| **Computational cost** | High (multi-model, multi-step)                    | Low (two-model, single-step)            |
| **Training stability** | Sensitive to hyperparameters; reward hacking risk | Generally stable and robust             |



### Why Does This Matter in Practice?

PPO-based RLHF requires loading four models into GPU memory simultaneously: the policy being trained, a frozen reference policy, a reward model, and a value network. Each carries its own optimizer state, gradient buffers, and update rules. The training loop samples from the live policy, scores responses with the reward model, and updates both the policy and value function — a complex, stateful pipeline with many failure modes and a large surface area for hyperparameter sensitivity.

DPO collapses this to a two-model setup (a trainable policy and a frozen reference) with a single forward pass per training step. There is no on-policy sampling loop, no reward model inference, and no PPO clipping logic. The gradient update is standard backpropagation through a binary cross-entropy loss — familiar, stable, and computationally efficient. For practitioners without access to large-scale compute infrastructure, this difference is often the deciding factor.



### Tradeoffs to Keep in Mind

DPO's simplicity is a genuine strength, but it comes with real limitations that practitioners should be aware of.

- **Static reference distribution.** DPO trains against a fixed, frozen reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. If the preference data was collected from a model whose output distribution differs significantly from $\pi_\theta$ at training time, the learned signal may be noisy or misaligned. Iterative and online DPO variants address this by periodically re-generating preference data from the current policy, keeping the training distribution on-policy.

- **Sensitivity to preference data quality.** Without an explicit reward model to aggregate and smooth over noisy annotations, the quality of the chosen–rejected pairs directly determines alignment quality. A single mislabeled or ambiguous preference pair has an outsized effect compared to PPO, where the reward model provides an additional layer of abstraction over raw human labels.

- **Limited flexibility for multi-objective alignment.** PPO-based methods can combine separate reward models for distinct objectives — helpfulness, safety, factuality — with tunable weights at training time. DPO folds all preference signal into a single scalar loss, making it harder to decompose or rebalance objectives without re-collecting preference data.

These limitations have motivated a growing family of extensions—including [Iterative DPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11456), [Online DPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08635), [IPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036), [KTO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01306), and [SimPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14734)—each targeting a specific weakness of the offline, single-reference setting. That DPO has nonetheless become a default alignment recipe in the open-source community speaks to how much the reduction in complexity is worth in practice.
